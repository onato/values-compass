NZ First’s West Coast contender wants banks to pay for climate resilience

Jamie Cleine knows mining won’t support the coast forever – but says it can’t afford to transition without making the most of the resources it’s got
by Fox Meyer 15/05/202618/05/2026

Former Buller mayor Jamie Cleine wants to bring resilience and wealth to the coast by unlocking its resource potential.

The newly-minted Parliamentary candidate thinks the competition for the West Coast-Tasman seat could become a bellwether for the wider election, and reckons he has a shot at beating candidates from the major parties as NZ First enjoys a populist surge.

Speaking to Newsroom, Cleine argues his good relationship with the minerals industry and experience in local government makes him the right advocate for the rural region, and thinks only NZ First understands the community properly.

The West Coast-Tasman electorate race doesn’t have an incumbent, following the news that National Party’s Maureen Pugh would retire at the election and former Labour MP Damien O’Connor has moved to Dunedin. Cleine finds himself up against National’s Katie Milne, Labour’s Rory Paterson, the Greens’ Andrew Beaumont and the Opportunity Party’s Cindie Uddstrom. 

Cleine reckons it’s one of a few electorates that NZ First has got a genuine shot at winning. “I think it’ll be a bit of a bellwether.”

To do that, Cleine will have to beat Milne, the former Federated Farmers president, and Paterson, the successor to O’Connor. “But I’m up for that.”

The West Coast is a unique region for many reasons, almost all of which come down to its geography. Tectonics here have created vast mineral opportunities as well as a thriving ecosystem – two draws which sometimes find themselves at odds. 

Over 80 percent of the land here is part of the conservation estate: untouchable for developers, more or less. For Cleine, it’s a promise of prosperity held behind bars. He doesn’t bring a “goodbye, Freddie” attitude to conservation, but wants resource extraction to live up to its full potential of supporting the community.

‘You don’t need to burn your bridges. Utilise the resources that are there now, and while they’re there, you’ve got a much stronger economy.’

Jamie Cleine, NZ First candidate

“The juxtaposition is that we’ve got this huge wealth of resources and things, and everyone else is telling us that we’re not allowed to use them while quite happily living off the back of modern technologies that are developed from resources just like that.”

When his candidacy was announced in March, NZ First also announced its long-awaited policy on changes to New Zealand’s mineral export royalties regime.

Under the party’s proposal, half of all royalties from mining must be returned directly to the community that hosts the mine. 

The idea came from a mayors, chairs and iwi group which Cleine was a part of, so to see it take shape as official party policy is “quite satisfying”.

Cleine spent his mayoralty advocating for resilience measures in Westport, including moving parts of the town away from the river, a constant source of flood risk for the town. Insurance premiums are rising, and earlier this year AA Insurance announced it was putting a freeze on new policies in Westport.

He thinks NZ First is the only party to take resilience issues seriously, pointing to initiatives like the regional infrastructure fund announced by Shane Jones in 2024. 

Some of this funding ought to come from the private sector as well, which Cleine thinks hasn’t been paying its fair share. Banks and insurance companies ought to up the ante as they have a financial stake in the game: “They benefit from risk reduction investment, and yet they’re not in any way compelled to contribute.”

Short-term solutions aren’t enough for Cleine, who wants to see long-term planning address a worsening climate. “It’s quite clear that there just needs to be greater investment into that resilience, rather than just responding to events as they happen.”

While it’s “quite clear” that the climate is changing, he won’t go so far as to say it’s entirely humanity’s fault. “The industrial age hasn’t helped.”

In 2021, the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change reported human actions were unequivocally the main driver of climate change, but his focus is less on the causes and more on the effects.

“I’m kind of pragmatic around New Zealand’s part in all that – it comes back to ‘focus your energy’. For me, it’s not on the emissions control side of stuff, it’s on, how do you learn to live with the effects?”

Cleine supports coal mining in the region, and also champions resilience efforts for flooding made worse by climate change. His view is that, in the grand scheme of things, a mine like Stockton is insignificant from a climate perspective. For a town like Westport, though, it’s financially crucial. 

Leaving coal in the ground would be tantamount to saying ‘no’ to infrastructure, pest control and community programmes.  

Cleine’s bigger concern about coal mines is what happens when they wrap up. If the Stockton mine’s fast-track bid is successful, and operations extend another 25 to 30 years, it puts a timeline on the industry’s contribution to local society.

Miners “know it’s not an infinite resource, so there is a need to transition our economy” – but nor do you need to immediately shut down a mine to do that. 

“You don’t need to burn your bridges. Utilise the resources that are there now, and while they’re there, you’ve got a much stronger economy,” Cleine says.

He thinks mining and a just transition to a non-mining economy can (or even must) coincide: “I totally refute the idea that we can’t have both at the same time.” 

Cleine’s candidacy comes as NZ First surges in the polls. A Talbot Mills poll released on Thursday put Winston Peters at 17 percent for preferred prime minister, only a few points behind Christopher Luxon. 

The populist party has long been a champion of the regions, and Cleine’s his choice to join them came because he felt they genuinely cared the most about the West Coast, and offered the best shot at making tangible changes.

Cleine says NZ First has been the only party to make a consistent effort to meet with West Coast mayors over the years. Even when it wasn’t in power, he could expect a visit every three to four months. 

Smaller parties are more free to challenge the status quo, while Labour and National “are sort of urban-centric – they’re almost frightened to take a hard position on anything for fear of upsetting a significant portion of the vote”.

The West Coast is sometimes seen in Wellington as a region looking for handouts, and he wants to change that.

“I think that just creates a little bit of a hard-done-by element. What I think, though, is the West Coast wants to champion the fact that actually, we’ve got some answers here.”

Those answers, mostly, have to do with its resources, which Cleine is happy to extract if it means a more prosperous and resilient coast.
