Methodology

How the questionnaire is built, how answers become scores, and what the result can and cannot tell you.

Purpose

Most voting-advice tools ask about policies: should tax X go up, should law Y be repealed. Answers to those questions shift with the news cycle and depend on knowing the local debate. This questionnaire instead asks about values: the principles a person falls back on when policies conflict. Values are more stable, apply in any country, and are what a party programme ultimately expresses. The profile it produces is meant to be handed to a separate matching step, together with the local parties and candidates, rather than to name a party itself.

The value model

The instrument measures twelve bipolar dimensions. Each has two poles and a score between them; neither pole is treated as better. The set draws on two established frameworks in psychology, Schwartz's theory of basic human values (for example openness to change versus conservation, and self-transcendence versus self-enhancement) and Moral Foundations Theory (care, fairness, loyalty, authority, sanctity, liberty), and on the axes political science uses to place parties: the economic left-right axis and the cultural axis often labelled GAL-TAN (green, alternative, libertarian versus traditional, authoritarian, nationalist).

Dimensions were chosen for three properties: they separate parties in practice, they can be phrased as principles rather than policies, and together they capture more than a single left-right line. Combinations matter more than any one axis. Someone who favours markets and solidarity sits in a different place from someone who favours markets and self-reliance, and multi-party systems have parties for both.

    Item design

    • Five statements per dimension, fifty in total. Several statements per dimension mean one odd answer moves the score by at most 20 points, and the spread of answers can be used as a consistency check.
    • Five-point agreement scale from strongly disagree to strongly agree, with a genuine neutral midpoint. This is the standard format for self-report inventories and is quick to answer on first reaction.
    • Reverse-keyed statements. In every dimension at least two statements are phrased from the opposite pole. Someone who tends to agree with everything, or to disagree with everything, therefore lands near the middle rather than at one end, and the pattern is flagged as mixed.
    • Principles, not policies. Statements avoid naming countries, institutions, parties or current events, so the same instrument works anywhere and does not age with the news.
    • Shuffled order. Statements are presented in a random order fixed for the session, with no two consecutive statements from the same dimension, so the person cannot see which dimension is being probed and answer for consistency rather than honestly.

    Scoring

    1. Each answer is a number from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree).
    2. Reverse-keyed statements are flipped (6 minus the answer), so that 5 always means "fully the first pole".
    3. The five keyed values in a dimension are averaged, giving a mean between 1 and 5.
    4. The mean is rescaled to a distance from the midpoint: (mean − 3) ÷ 2 × 100, shown on the page as a strength from 0 to 100 towards one pole, for example "55 towards Security". In the JSON export the same value is written as a signed number from −100 to +100, positive meaning the first-named pole, because that is the form the matching arithmetic and the research prompt use.

    Strength labels are attached by the distance from the midpoint, whichever pole it points to:

    DistanceLabelMeaning
    0 to 14balancedNo clear leaning; roughly neutral, or agreement split across the poles.
    15 to 44leansA mild but real preference.
    45 to 74clearlyA firm preference on most statements.
    75 to 100stronglyConsistent strong agreement with one pole.

    Consistency. The standard deviation of the five keyed values shows whether the person answered the dimension coherently. A deviation above 1.2 means they agreed with statements from both poles, and the dimension is marked mixed. A mixed score is still reported but should carry less weight in matching.

    Importance. How far you lean is not the same as how much a dimension decides your vote, so after the statements you can mark each dimension as mattering less, mattering, or mattering a lot. That multiplies the dimension's weight by 0.5, 1 or 1.5 in the priority order and in matching. Left unanswered, every dimension matters equally and strength of leaning alone sets the order. You can also name concerns outside the twelve dimensions; they go into your summary and the research prompt, and are never folded into a score.

    Priorities. Dimensions are ordered by distance from the midpoint multiplied by importance, so the order doubles as the weighting used in matching.

    Reading the profile

    The JSON profile contains, for each dimension, the score, the strength label, the pole leaned towards, and the consistency flag, plus the priority order and a summary paragraph. The summary is generated from the scores in the first person and can be edited, because a person is the final authority on their own values; the edited text is what goes into the export.

    Limitations

    • It is self-report. It measures what people say they value, which can differ from how they vote or behave.
    • It has not been psychometrically validated. The dimensions and thresholds are grounded in established theory, but the specific statements have not been tested on a large sample for reliability or factor structure. Treat scores as indicative, not precise.
    • Dimensions are correlated. Solidarity, Regulation and Environment tend to move together, as do Tradition, National and Security. They are kept separate because parties split them in different ways, but the profile is not ten independent measurements.
    • Country-neutral wording cuts both ways. It travels well, but a local issue that dominates an election may not map neatly onto any dimension.
    • Values are not the whole vote. Competence, trust, coalition arithmetic and single issues also matter. The profile is one input to a decision, not the decision.

    How parties are scored

    The results page compares your profile with the New Zealand parties in Parliament, plus TOP, scored on the same twelve dimensions. The scores are our assessment, made by reading each party's current policy pages and manifesto, the 2023 coalition agreements, recorded party votes and second-reading speeches on marker bills, the governing record, and statutory assessments such as the Climate Change Commission's monitoring reports and the Attorney-General's Bill of Rights reports. Expert datasets such as the Global Party Survey and the Manifesto Project are used only as cross-checks, because they predate the current term.

    Each party's score on each dimension answers one question: if this party governed alone, which way would it pull, and how hard? Programme comes first and record second; where they diverge we score the programme and say so. Anchors keep parties comparable: ±100 means the pole is a defining purpose of the party, ±60 a clear and consistent direction, ±30 a lean, 0 balanced or silent. Each score carries a confidence: high when programme and record agree across at least two source types, medium when one strong source or several thinner ones point the same way, low when evidence is thin, vague or contradictory. A party with no discernible position gets no score rather than a forced one.

    Matching is a weighted distance. Each dimension is weighted by how strongly you lean on it, with a small floor so a balanced profile still produces a ranking, and halved where your answers were mixed. Alignment is 100 minus that distance as a share of the maximum possible, so 100 per cent means a party sits exactly where you do on every dimension you care about. Where a party or candidate has no score on some dimensions, those count as unknown: the headline figure is pulled toward 50 in proportion to the weight you put on them, and the figure over the scored dimensions alone is shown beside it.

    Every cell on the results page shows its rationale, confidence and dated sources, and the source documents are archived so the scores can be re-checked when parties publish new material. The sources page lists the catalogue of admitted sources, the reasons others were excluded, and every archived document. If you disagree with a score, the research prompt gives you an independent route: hand it to an agent of your choice and compare. Coalition commitments demanded by one party are attributed to that party in the rationale; they count for the lead party only where it adopted them as government policy. Where a party's evidence pulls in both directions on a dimension, for example liberal on speech but hard on crime, the cell is marked as a split position: the score still stands as the net direction, but it counts as less certain in matching and the rationale explains both pulls.

    How candidates are scored

    Where an electorate has been researched, its candidates appear below the party ranking. Individual positions are hard to establish in New Zealand: most parliamentary votes are party votes, and new candidates have no record at all. So a candidate's cell is scored from their own statements only where such statements exist, with a source for each. Every other cell inherits the party's score with its confidence lowered one step, and says so. Candidates whose party is not scored are scored only on what they have said themselves, and are otherwise left unscored, which also lowers their alignment's certainty rather than their alignment. The field is provisional until nominations close on 8 October 2026.

    Privacy

    The page has no server. Answers, scores and the summary are stored only in this browser's local storage and leave it only when you copy or download them.

    All statements

    Every statement, grouped by dimension, with the pole that agreeing with it counts towards. Statements phrased from the second pole are reverse-keyed: agreeing with them counts towards that pole, so the two kinds check each other.
